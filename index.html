01 · Overview

Why severe pediatric trauma / RTA-related orthopedic injury

Of nine long-term pediatric physiotherapy conditions researched for this project, trauma is the only one with consistently strong, hospital-level Indian evidence; everything else (JIA, Perthes disease, DMD, pediatric spinal cord injury, osteosarcoma-related rehab) is data-sparse or has an outright reporting gap.

In plain EnglishRoad accidents and falls put more Indian children in physiotherapy, with better documentation, than any other long-term condition considered, so it's the safest, best-evidenced starting point for a product aimed at a broad population rather than a rare condition.

India accounts for roughly 6% of global vehicular accidents despite having only about 1% of the world's motor vehicles. In one Delhi hospital study, road traffic accidents caused 59.47% of male pediatric trauma cases, and ages 11–15 were the most affected group; of all pediatric trauma patients at that hospital, 26.48% were polytrauma (2+ body regions involved). With the condition chosen, the open question this analysis answers is: of the four body regions relevant to a physiotherapy movement taxonomy (Arm, Back, Leg, Neck), which is most affected?

02 · Methodology

Three datasets, one keyword-extraction pipeline, two hypotheses

In plain EnglishOne dataset is raw patient-level trauma registry data we can filter and test directly. The other two are already-published, aggregated tables from independent hospital studies, used as baselines to sanity-check whatever the raw data shows.
TITCO-I

16,000-row anonymized Indian trauma registry (AIIMS Delhi, KEM Mumbai, LTMGH Mumbai, SSKM Kolkata; 2013–2015; CC-BY-4.0). Patient-level; filtered to age ≤17 and mechanism-of-injury = road traffic, then body region extracted via regex keyword matching across 47 free-text injury columns.

Delhi (all-cause)

Published table, n=100 children (6 months–12 years), Sural & Verma 2015. All injury mechanisms combined (falls 79%, RTA 8%, other 13%): a baseline, not RTA-specific.

South India (all-cause)

Published table, n=438 patients / 732 injuries, Elangovan & Munnuswamy 2025. All mechanisms combined, with two-wheeler RTA as the single largest at 29.5%, closer to RTA-dominant than Delhi, still not RTA-isolated.

Pipeline: load & verify → filter pediatric RTA → keyword-extract body region (Arm/Leg/Neck/Back, polytrauma-aware) → Wilson 95% CI per region → chi-square goodness-of-fit test (H1) → cross-dataset chi-square validation against Delhi and South India (H2) → visualization → design-direction synthesis. Full statistical notebook: interactive_physiotherapy_for_kids_analysis_02.py.

03 · Body Region Signal

What the two confirmed baselines already show

Both independently published, all-cause Indian pediatric trauma cohorts agree on one thing before any TITCO-specific test is even run: the upper limb (arm) is the single most affected region.

Fig 3.1: Semicircular radial bars (0 to 60% scale). Arm vs. Leg share of all-cause pediatric musculoskeletal injury, Delhi (n=100) and South India (n=438). Neck and Back are excluded from this comparison: Delhi only reports a combined "Head & Neck" category, and neither study isolates Back the way this project's keyword extraction does for TITCO; a real category-mapping limitation, not an oversight.
Key findingArm leads in both independent cohorts (53% vs. 33% in Delhi and 22.8% vs. 20.8% in South India), despite the two studies having different mechanism mixes, different sample sizes, and different regions of India. That consistency is a real signal, though see Section 04 for how the RTA-specific TITCO test nuances this picture once it's isolated to road-traffic cases specifically.
04 · Hypothesis Testing

Two formal hypotheses, tested against the real TITCO-I data

Run against the real TITCO-I limited sample (1,000 patients, a random subsample of the full 16,000-row registry), filtered to pediatric (≤17) road-traffic-injury cases, n=60.

Headline result: more nuanced than the baselines alone suggestedThe RTA-specific distribution is significantly non-uniform (p=0.040); something real is going on. But within TITCO's own RTA subset, Leg edges narrowly ahead of Arm (16.7% vs 15.0%, n=60), not the clean Arm-leads confirmation DD-01 was written expecting. The two regions' 95% confidence intervals overlap heavily (Arm: 8.1–26.1%; Leg: 9.3–28.0%), so this specific gap isn't statistically decisive on its own. Cross-validated against both baselines, TITCO's Arm/Leg balance is statistically consistent with (not different from) the Arm-leading pattern in Delhi and South India. Honest read: real evidence of a skew toward the limbs over neck/back, without a statistically clean answer on whether Arm or Leg leads specifically in RTA cases at this sample size.

Falls dominating over RTA matches the Delhi baseline's own mechanism mix (falls 79%, RTA 8%), a small internal consistency check that this sample behaves as expected. 3 of the 60 RTA patients (5%) matched more than one body region (polytrauma).

06 · Practitioner Voices

Four physiotherapist interviews, triangulating the data

Numbers say what the published cohorts show; practitioners explain what actually happens in a session. All four interviews were conducted by call. Two (Hency Parmar, Dr. Rakesh) are reconstructed from a solo recap rather than a direct recording, with inaudible sections flagged rather than guessed at in the source transcripts; Dr. Pranav's is a direct transcript of the actual call, the most reliable source of the four.

"Mostly kids with fractures sustained while playing… the most fracture/injury-prone area in this age group is the elbow and forearm… the tendon that attaches the forearm to the elbow joint is usually where the actual problem sits."
Dr. Vishaka Pawar
Physiotherapist, 6–7 years' experience
Also endorsed automated progress-tracking ("Definitely yes") and named pain tolerance / resistance as the core adherence barrier; distraction helps, but "the kid still has to use the equipment."
"That can be great! That can be absolutely great… Especially for the pediatric population, we need to track every small growth and development of the child. Every month there is either progress or some kind of problem that arises."
Dr. Pranav
Physiotherapist, 6–7 years' experience
Only interview that's a direct call transcript, not a solo-recap reconstruction. Named wrist, knee, and ankle fractures as the most common cases; said range of motion is the "absolute first priority" before strength training, since "movement must be initiated" first. Leans toward clinical/outpatient setup for visibility and specialty growth, but confirmed pediatric home setups need minimal equipment. Session pricing: ~₹2,000 (suburban), ~₹700–800 (lower-income), ~₹300–500 (government).
"Repetition, specifically… if a product could let the kid do the exercise on their own and track that progress automatically, it would cut out a lot of that repeated manual work."
Hency Parmar
Physiotherapist, 3–4 years' experience, government hospital background
Reconstructed from solo recap, not a direct recording. Home vs. clinical setup described as roughly equally common, split mainly by affordability.
"[Largely inaudible; legible fragments:] 'track, progress… help… patient… track progress'; gist: agreement that patient-side tracking would help."
Dr. Rakesh
Physiotherapist, 10–15 years' experience, private clinic
Source recording was heavily garbled; most of this interview is only partially legible and is flagged as such rather than filled in. Confirmed: YouTube videos are the default home-exercise guide today.
Convergent findingAll four physiotherapists, independently and in separate calls, endorsed a device that tracks how a child interacts with it and syncs that data back periodically, specifically to reduce how often they need to manually check in. Dr. Pranav went furthest, tying it directly to pediatric care itself: "we need to track every small growth and development of the child." This is validated demand, not a hypothesis to be tested.
07 · Design Directions

From evidence to design direction

Seven directions, each traceable to a specific dataset or interview. If the underlying evidence changes (most notably once the TITCO-specific hypothesis results come in), the direction it supports should be revisited.

DD-01 · Baseline data + Practitioner interviews + TITCO-I (revisited)
Prioritize Arm: specifically elbow/forearm
Source: Delhi (n=100) & South India (n=438) baselines, both showing Upper limb as the largest category; corroborated firsthand by Dr. Vishaka Pawar naming elbow/forearm fractures from falls-while-playing as the most common presenting case in exactly this age band, and by Dr. Pranav independently naming wrist fractures among the most common post-surgical cases in this age group.
  • Two independent published cohorts and two practitioner interviews converge on Arm before any TITCO-specific test is run: four independent sources.
  • Revisited now that the real TITCO-I RTA-specific test is in (Section 04), per this document's own rule that directions get revisited when their evidence changes: the result is more nuanced than expected. TITCO's own RTA subset (n=60) has Leg narrowly ahead of Arm (16.7% vs 15.0%), though the two are statistically indistinguishable at this sample size (overlapping 95% CIs), and TITCO's Arm/Leg balance is statistically consistent with (not different from) both baselines' Arm-leading pattern.
  • Net call: Arm stays the prioritized region, on weight of evidence (2 baselines + 2 interviews vs. one small, statistically-inconclusive RTA subsample), but this is now a judgment call on balance of evidence, not a clean statistical sweep. Worth re-running against the full 16,000-row TITCO-I file (this used the 1,000-row limited sample) before treating it as fully settled.
DD-02 · Practitioner interviews
Target elbow flexion-extension and forearm pronation-supination: range of motion before strength
Source: Dr. Vishaka Pawar, tendon at the forearm-elbow junction named as the typical problem site. Sequencing refined by Dr. Pranav: for upper-limb post-trauma cases, range of motion is the "absolute first priority… movement must be initiated" before strength training begins.
  • Gives the product's primary sensing axis a specific anatomical target, not a generic "arm movement" brief.
  • Matches the clinical assessment process described in both interviews: checking strength, range of motion, and balance of the affected joint.
  • Session/loop structure should sequence ROM-building movement before strength-loaded resistance, not treat both as interchangeable from session one.
DD-03 · All four practitioner interviews
Automated progress tracking is validated demand
Source: Hency Parmar, Dr. Rakesh, Dr. Vishaka Pawar, Dr. Pranav: independently, all four endorsed a device that tracks usage data and reports back periodically. Dr. Pranav's reaction was the most emphatic ("That can be absolutely great") and tied it specifically to pediatric care, where monthly growth/development needs consistent tracking.
  • Reduces repeated manual check-ins, the single most-cited workload driver across interviews.
  • Reporting cadence should match existing practice: weekly to monthly, not real-time clinical supervision (see DD-07).
  • Now a 4-for-4 convergent finding across every practitioner interviewed, not a majority; the strongest-evidenced direction in this set.
DD-04 · Practitioner interviews
Gamification as a secondary layer, not a replacement
Source: Dr. Vishaka Pawar: kids' lower pain tolerance drives resistance and disengagement, but "the kid still has to use the equipment." Corroborated by Dr. Pranav: children are genuinely "scared at the beginning" and early sessions are difficult, but even low-tech centers already manage this with soft toys, storybooks, and coloring books rather than avoiding the exercise itself.
  • Distraction/gamification should support adherence, not obscure or replace the core therapeutic mechanism.
  • Design the reward/game loop around session completion, not around masking that the exercise is happening.
  • This device's story-on-completion mechanic is a direct digital analogue of the storybook/toy distraction already used in practice today.
DD-05 · Practitioner interviews
Anchor sensing to clinically trusted tools
Source: Dr. Vishaka Pawar naming goniometers, resistance bands, and pressure balls as the real-world equipment baseline for elbow/forearm cases. Dr. Pranav independently named pressure/strength-measuring devices with built-in resistance, distinguishing them from a plain dynamometer ("a dynamometer just measures strength; it does not provide resistance").
  • Goniometer-class angle sensing and resistance-band-style loading are the components to digitize, not reinvent.
  • Keeps the product legible to a practitioner reviewing session data: it should read as an instrumented version of tools they already trust.
  • Worth flagging: Dr. Pranav's ideal device both measures force and provides resistance. This project's current hardware decision provides resistance mechanically (graduated-force spring plungers) without electronic force measurement, after an earlier force-sensor approach was removed for reliability/complexity reasons: a real, acknowledged trade-off against this direction, not an oversight.
DD-06 · Practitioner interviews
Design for a 5–8 week engagement arc
Source: Dr. Vishaka Pawar: critical-condition physiotherapy recovery typically runs 6–8 weeks. Corroborated by Dr. Pranav: baseline rehabilitation takes "around five to six weeks," after which training/conditioning continues.
  • Adherence and motivation mechanics need to sustain interest across weeks, not just a single session.
  • Progression/difficulty curves should be paced against this real clinical window.
  • Two independent practitioners give overlapping ranges (5–6 and 6–8 weeks): a 5–8 week arc covers both.
DD-07 · Practitioner interviews
Support home and clinical deployment equally
Source: Dr. Vishaka Pawar & Hency Parmar: setup choice is financially, not clinically, determined; progress reporting already happens on a periodic (weekly to monthly) cadence. Dr. Pranav adds nuance and pricing: he personally leans toward clinical/outpatient setups for visibility and specialty growth, but confirms pediatric home setups are genuinely viable ("don't need a lot of heavy equipment, just certain mats and therapy balls"), with concrete session pricing that explains the financial split: roughly ₹2,000/session (suburban, well-off families), ₹700–800 (lower-income families), ₹300–500 (government setups).
  • No functional compromise should be needed between home and clinic use.
  • Data-sync model should match the existing periodic reporting rhythm rather than assume constant supervision.
  • Pricing spread (₹300–2,000/session) confirms affordability, not clinical necessity, is the real driver of home vs. clinic choice; reinforces designing one product that works equally well in both settings rather than two variants.
08 · Limitations & Data Gaps

What this analysis can't yet claim

  • TITCO-I results used the 1,000-row limited sample, not the full 16,000-row file. The limited file is itself a random subsample of the full registry (per its own documentation): real data, correctly analyzed, but a smaller and slightly less representative base than the full file would give. The pediatric-RTA subset actually tested was n=60.
  • TITCO's RTA-specific Arm-vs-Leg ordering is not statistically decisive. Leg (16.7%) narrowly edges Arm (15.0%) in raw counts, but their 95% confidence intervals overlap substantially; this sample size can't cleanly distinguish the two. DD-01 has been revisited to reflect this honestly rather than overstating the TITCO result as a clean confirmation.
  • Delhi and South India are all-cause, not RTA-isolated baselines. RTA was only 8% of the Delhi cohort's mechanism mix (vs. 29.5% for two-wheeler RTA alone in South India): both are directional context, not a substitute for the RTA-specific TITCO result.
  • Small, single-site published samples. Delhi (n=100, one hospital) and South India (n=438, one district) each represent a single site: real, peer-reviewed data, but not nationally representative on their own.
  • Interview methodology is mixed across the four transcripts. Hency Parmar and Dr. Rakesh are rebuilt from a solo spoken recap (transcribed afterward) rather than a recording of the actual call, with inaudible sections flagged rather than filled in. Dr. Pranav's is a direct transcript of the actual call, the most reliable source of the four.
  • Four interviews is still a small, non-random sample of practitioners. Even where two practitioners now corroborate a direction (DD-01, DD-02, DD-04, DD-05, DD-06, DD-07), this reflects convergence across a handful of individual clinicians' experience, not a surveyed consensus.
